---
title: The Voidness of Claude
date: 2026-10-30
description: Is Claude actually "thinking"?
---

I ask another question, and a burst of text shows up on my screen.

_Loop @Nancy in on the upcoming call with Oliver; make sure that all tests are ready to go for the new version shipping tomorrow._

This is the technology that’s quickly making its way across corporate America, apparently encouraging CEOs to question why they need employees in the first place.

On the one hand, it’s an incredible feat. It’s a computer that’s talking to me. It’s triaging my emails, it’s doing the heavy lifting on Middle Place’s codebase, and it’s monitoring other Slack channels for updates that are relevant to my team’s work. Yet at a time when humanity is questioning what intelligence means, I find myself increasingly seeing the limits of the technology.

Sure, it’s capable of producing new math proofs and making advancements in science. In some respects, its capabilities are beyond what most people thought was possible for AI. Two years ago, the discussion was about how LLMs were just reading all of the text ever written by humanity and regurgitating it in a new way. Now, we’re talking about how LLMs are working in swarms at the frontier of humanity’s achievements in math and software engineering. The difference is astounding.

And yet, as I use Claude (and other AI tools) more in my day-to-day work, I increasingly see the emptiness of thought in its output. Perhaps this is because I work in product, which by design requires intensive decision-making and thought. After I draft PRDs for a while, my brain _hurts_. It’s hard work to do because I’m constantly weighing various options and am tasked with making the _right_ call. Claude, by contrast, treats a PRD as a page to fill with an undifferentiated wall of text, with no part more important than any other.

There’s no meaning to a Claude-written product document (e.g., a PRD) – it’s a statistical output, a web of probabilities based on its fine-tuning that results in the document. There’s no _thought_ behind it because Claude simply has no experience, no care, no understanding of… well, anything. Don’t get me wrong – it “knows” how various parts of language are connected, which words are closer in meaning, and which ones are further away. But Claude doesn’t know what it’s like to have agency – it doesn’t know what it’s like to have life, goals, or fears. And it has no sense of accountability if things go wrong.

I think that’s what disturbs me about seeing a Claude-written decision on how to move a product forward – it’s making a decision on paper, but behind it, it’s completely devoid of care or thought. Unlike a human, it didn’t spend longer generating tokens for the decision-making paragraph than for the introduction, and it never fretted over what to say. Sure, it could _simulate_ worrying about what to say, but it could never _actually_ worry about what to say – because generating text is in its nature. Its very “thoughts” are ingrained in the text that it produces. There’s no external “thinking” beyond the text – the text is the “thinking” itself.

For humans, writing is laborious and time-consuming; for an LLM, generating text is its very being. It’s impossible for its thoughts to exist without generating text – I’d compare it to breathing for humans: something so easy for an LLM to do. That’s important, because language models like Claude have a tendency to generate more, more, more. Ask Claude how to improve your app or website, and you’ll find yourself endlessly adding new features, new code, new pages, and new buttons. Making good decisions is hard, and Claude will rarely make a truly insightful point – it’ll never say “Actually, I thought about it, and I think this page feels too busy” or “I think we need to rearchitect the codebase because right now it’s a mess.” Surely, that’s in part by design – imagine if Claude upon entering your workspace endlessly criticized your existing work and constantly offered to completely redo it.

Of course, one could argue that this is simply the result of the frontier labs’ fine-tuning. However, I’d argue that, despite the endless number of open models out there, no lab has produced one model that shows interesting and thoughtful decision-making abilities. That highlights the fundamental shortcomings of the technology. It’s incredibly good at predicting the next sequence of tokens – no, wait, more than that. It’s incredibly good at finding the solution to a defined task. But what it’s terrible at is creating great work in an open space where no problem is defined. Claude isn’t going to come up with the next groundbreaking product because making products requires taste. And taste is something that distinctly requires refinement, not slop generation.

Now, what if I got an LLM to endlessly think, far more than anyone ever has, about new startup ideas – could that ever rise to the level of human thinking? I’d still argue that no, it couldn’t. This isn’t to say that LLMs could **never** come up with a good idea. It’s just that, because LLMs are completely devoid of any accountability, responsibility, or personal identity, any suggestion they come up with would not be trustworthy. For example, is the LLM suggesting this because it “thinks” it’s a good idea or simply because that idea was the most likely next collection of tokens? And of course, if the LLM suggests the wrong idea and the business fails, coming back to tell it later would only get you an “Oh, I’m so sorry about that.” Unlike a human who is actually thinking, an LLM that only simulates thinking has no accountability or responsibility for the outcome.

What’s particularly disturbing about this is that the AI gives the impression that it _is_ doing this thinking and producing genuine and insightful thoughts. For example, Claude will use phrases like “I really thought about this, and I think \_\_\_\_.” But again, behind it, there’s a complete void of actual thought.

So what to make of all of this? Claude’s incredible at smooth-talking, writing an apology, writing things that need to _appear_ polished and pristine. But under the hood, it’s not capable of genuine insight – of genuine ownership of a problem. Its “decision-making” abilities are essentially a facade, a masquerade so good that sometimes it produces 100% accurate responses to problems no human has ever solved. But just because it produced the response doesn’t mean that it actually thought about the solution. It’s akin to a student who turns in his answers at the end of the test without showing any work – who never actually thought about how to get the answer, only _appeared_ to think about it. That’s not the person you would entrust to own a project or lead a company. Because that person needs to be someone who can back up the decisions they make, who can explain why they came to a particular answer, and who has personal ownership in the outcome. So if you find yourself giving over significant decision making abilities to Claude, _stop_ – think for yourself. After all, that’s the beauty of being someone with a brain, someone who can produce genuinely new ideas with stakes in what they do. Get Claude to implement as much as you can. But don’t let Claude become the thinker for what you are working on.
