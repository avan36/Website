Wine for beer drinkers: start with the beer you like

Updated November 2026

If you know what you like in a beer, you know more about wine than you think. Bitterness, sourness, body, sweetness and roast all have close cousins in a glass of wine. Start from your favorite beer and the wine list gets a lot shorter.

Why beer and wine palates overlap

Beer comes from grain and hops, wine from grapes, but your palate notices the same handful of things in both: how bitter or grippy a drink is, how tart, how heavy, how sweet, and whether there's toast or vanilla in the background. Here's how they line up.

Bitterness and tannin

Hop bitterness is a taste. Tannin, which comes mostly from grape skins, seeds and oak barrels, is more of a texture: the drying grip you feel on your gums after a sip of young red wine. They do a similar job, though. Both cut through richness and keep a drink from feeling flat. If you like a firm, bitter IPA, a red with some grip may suit you better than a soft one.

Sourness and acidity

Every wine has acidity, but some are noticeably bright and mouthwatering. If you enjoy sour beers or the snap of a crisp lager, high-acid whites and dry sparkling wines will feel familiar.

Body

The gap between a light lager and an imperial stout is like the gap between a Pinot Grigio and a ripe Zinfandel. In wine, body tracks closely with alcohol: riper, stronger wines feel heavier in the mouth, just as a 10% beer feels thicker than a 4% one.

Sweetness

Most wines on a restaurant list are dry. If you like malty, slightly sweet beers, look for off-dry styles such as some Rieslings and Chenin Blancs, or for fruit-forward reds that taste ripe even when they have almost no sugar.

Oak and roast

Barrel-aged and roasty beers share a lot with oak-aged wine. Oak can give wine vanilla, toast and spice, and some bold reds pick up mocha or dark chocolate notes too.

How to read a wine list as a beer drinker

A wine list looks like a wall of names, but most follow a simple pattern.

  • Find the sections. Lists are usually split into sparkling, white, rosé and red. Some run from light to full within each section, a bit like a tap list that runs from lager to stout.
  • Grape or place. Wines are named either by grape (Pinot Noir, Sauvignon Blanc) or by where they're from (Chablis, Rioja). European wines usually use the place. A few translations help: Chablis is Chardonnay, often unoaked; white Sancerre is Sauvignon Blanc; red Burgundy is Pinot Noir; Rioja is mostly Tempranillo; Chianti is mostly Sangiovese.
  • Check the alcohol. If the list shows it, higher alcohol usually means a riper, fuller wine.
  • Start by the glass. Wines by the glass let you try a new grape without committing to a bottle, and some places will pour a small taste first.
  • Talk in beer. You don't need wine vocabulary. "I usually drink hazy IPAs. What's fruity and aromatic?" or "I like stouts. What's big and smooth?" gives staff plenty to work with.

A quick word on cider and spirits

Cider sits between beer and wine. It's fermented fruit, like wine, but usually nearer beer in strength, and often sparkling. If you like crisp lagers, try a dry sparkling cider. If you like sours, a tart, dry cider is a short step.

Spirits translate mostly through oak and smoke. Many barrel-aged stouts spend time in bourbon barrels, so bourbon is an easy bridge, with vanilla and caramel notes from new charred oak. Fans of smoky beers like rauchbier might try a peated Scotch whisky. Spirits are far stronger than beer or wine, so a small pour tells you all you need to know.

Questions

What wine should a beer drinker try first?

Start from the beer you like most. Crisp lager drinkers often get on with Sauvignon Blanc or Albariño, hazy IPA fans tend to like aromatic whites such as Riesling, and stout drinkers usually take to bold reds like Malbec or Zinfandel.

If I like IPA, what wine should I try?

For a hazy, juicy IPA, try an aromatic white like Riesling, Gewürztraminer or Torrontés. For a bitter West Coast IPA, try a New Zealand Sauvignon Blanc for the citrus, or a red with some grip like Cabernet Franc.

Is there a wine that tastes like stout?

No wine tastes like stout, but bold reds such as Malbec, Zinfandel and Syrah share its weight and dark, rich flavors. For a sweet stout, a small glass of Port is the closest match.

Is tannin the same as bitterness?

Not quite. Bitterness is a taste, like the hops in an IPA. Tannin is a texture: the drying grip red wine leaves on your gums. They do a similar job, which is why fans of bitter beer can enjoy reds with some tannin.

What wine is closest to a sour beer?

A high-acid one. Try a dry Riesling, a Vinho Verde, or a dry sparkling wine such as Champagne or Cava. They have the same bright, mouthwatering tartness.
