How to rate beer: a simple scale and tasting notes

Updated November 2026

Rating beer well has nothing to do with a fancy palate. It comes down to noticing the same few things in the same order, and using your scale the same way next month as you do today.

What you're actually judging

Every beer breaks down into five parts. You don't need to be an expert in any of them, you just need to notice them.

  • Look. Color, clarity and head. Hazy doesn't mean flawed, since some styles are meant to be cloudy. Looks matter least, so don't let a pretty pour carry the score.
  • Smell. Much of what we call flavor is really aroma, so this is where most of the information is.
  • Taste. Notice the balance between sweet malt and bitter hops, and how the flavor moves from the first touch to the swallow.
  • Mouthfeel. Body from thin to full, carbonation from soft to prickly, and any warmth from the alcohol.
  • Finish. What stays after you swallow. Is it dry or sweet, short or long, clean or lingering?

A one-minute tasting routine

This fits into the time it takes your friends to read the menu.

  1. Pour into a clean glass and leave some head. Hold it up to the light for a few seconds.
  2. Swirl gently and smell it. Try to name one or two things, even vague ones like bready or citrus.
  3. Take a proper sip, not a polite one. Let it cover your tongue before you swallow.
  4. Breathe out through your nose after swallowing. A lot of flavor shows up here.
  5. Take a second sip to check your first impression, and pay attention to mouthfeel and finish.
  6. Score it and write one line about why.

A beer rating scale that means something

A score is only useful if a 7 means the same thing every time. Here is one way to use a 1 to 10 scale in half steps, the same range busy beer uses.

ScoreWhat it meansWould you order it again?
1 to 2Flawed or unpleasant. Off flavors, or you didn't finish it.No
3 to 4Drinkable, but you wouldn't choose it.Only if nothing else is on
5 to 6Fine. Nothing wrong, nothing memorable. A 6 is a decent beer you'd accept if offered.Maybe
7Good. Clean and enjoyable.Yes
8Very good. You'd look for it again.Yes, on purpose
9Excellent. One of your favorites in its style.Yes, and you'd recommend it
10One of the best beers you've had. Save it for a handful.It sets the bar for others

Half steps separate beers within a band. A 7.5 is a beer you liked more than a 7 but wouldn't go out of your way for.

Let your score measure how much you enjoyed it, and use notes for the rest. A well-made beer in a style you dislike can fairly get a 5, with a note that says "well made, just not for me".

Words to describe beer taste

Most beer flavors fall into six families. Start with the family, then get specific. These are the same six beer flavor tags busy beer uses, and its taste profile maps each one on a 0 to 100 axis.

Yeast adds its own notes: banana and clove in German wheat beers, pepper and fruit in saisons. For mouthfeel, try thin, full, creamy, prickly or warming. For the finish: dry, clean, lingering or astringent, that drying grip you get from strong tea.

Off flavors worth knowing

  • Butter or butterscotch. Usually a fermentation problem.
  • Wet cardboard. Oxidation, from an old or badly stored beer.
  • Skunky. Light-struck, from beer left in sunlight. Common in clear and green bottles.
  • Cooked corn. Mostly found in pale lagers.

A filled-in example: Hazy IPA, 6.5%. Look: pale orange, opaque, white head. Smell: mango, orange peel, a little pine. Taste: juicy, light sweetness, soft bitterness. Mouthfeel: medium body, gentle carbonation. Finish: short and clean. Score: 7.5. Would order again, wouldn't hunt it down.

Common mistakes

  • Rating the occasion, not the beer. A sunny patio and good company make everything taste better. Would you score it the same at home on a quiet Tuesday?
  • Anchoring on ABV, price or rarity. Stronger isn't better, and hard to find isn't the same as good. A crisp 4.5% pilsner can deserve a higher score than a 12% stout.
  • Rating with a blown-out palate. Very bitter, sour or strong beers linger, and whatever comes next tastes flat. If you're trying a few, go from lighter to stronger, with water in between.
  • Judging the style instead of the beer. If you don't like sours, a great sour may never be a 9 for you. That's fine, just say so in your notes.
  • Scoring a stale beer. Hop aroma fades with time, so an old IPA can taste dull. Check the canning date if there is one.

How to keep ratings consistent over time

  • Pick a few anchor beers. Give two or three beers you know well fixed scores. When you're unsure about a new one, ask whether it's better or worse than an anchor.
  • Always write one line. Future you will want to know why something got a 6.
  • Check your spread. If almost everything lands at 7 or 8, your scale has shrunk. Use the whole range.
  • Update when you change your mind. Batches vary and palates change. Re-rating a beer isn't cheating.

Questions

What is a good beer rating out of 10?

On a 1 to 10 scale, a 7 usually means good and worth ordering again, and an 8 means you'd look for it. Keep 9 and 10 rare. A 5 or 6 is fine but forgettable.

How do you describe the taste of beer?

Work through smell, taste, mouthfeel and finish, and name one or two things for each. Start with a flavor family, such as hoppy, malty, sour, bitter, sweet or roasty, then get specific: citrus or pine, caramel or toast, coffee or chocolate.

What are the steps of tasting beer?

Look at it, smell it, taste it, notice the mouthfeel, then pay attention to the finish. Some people remember it as see, swirl, sniff, sip and savor. Smell before you sip, since much of what we call flavor is really aroma.

What is the difference between hoppy and bitter?

Hoppy describes the aroma and flavor hops bring, like citrus, pine, tropical fruit or grass. Bitter is the sharp taste on your tongue. They often go together, but a hazy IPA can be very hoppy and only gently bitter.

Should beer be served ice cold?

Not always. Very cold temperatures mute aroma and flavor. Light lagers are fine well chilled, while ales, stouts and strong beers usually show more character a little warmer. If a beer tastes of nothing, give it a few minutes.

Should I drink from a glass when rating beer?

If you can, yes. Pouring releases aroma and lets you see the color and head. Drinking straight from a can or bottle hides most of the smell, which is a big part of flavor.
