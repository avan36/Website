Beer styles, explained by taste

Updated November 2026

A style name tells you where a beer comes from. It doesn't always tell you what it tastes like. This guide sorts the common styles by the flavor you'd actually notice, so you can find your lane and branch out from there.

Brewers stretch styles all the time, so treat the descriptions and strength ranges here as rough guides, and the menu or label as the final word.

Crisp and clean

This is where most people start. Lager yeast keeps fruity notes low, so you taste the malt and hops plainly.

  • Pale lager and helles. Light, bready and gently sweet, with low bitterness. Helles, from Munich, leans toward soft malt.
  • Pilsner. Crisper and more bitter, with floral, spicy or herbal hops. Czech versions are rounder; German ones are drier and sharper.
  • Kölsch. From Cologne. Fermented with ale yeast, then cold-conditioned like a lager, so it's clean with a faint fruitiness.

Hoppy and bitter

Hops bring aroma, such as citrus, pine and tropical fruit, and they bring bitterness. These styles mix the two in different amounts, which is why two IPAs can taste nothing alike.

  • Pale ale. Citrus or pine hops over a biscuity malt base, with moderate bitterness. A good first step into hoppy beer.
  • IPA. More hops and firmer bitterness. The classic West Coast style is clear, dry and resinous.
  • Hazy IPA (NEIPA). Cloudy, soft and juicy, with tropical fruit aroma and much gentler bitterness. Oats or wheat give it a smoother body.
  • Double IPA (DIPA). More hops and more alcohol, which can add warmth and a touch of sweetness.

Malty and sweet

Malt gives bread, toast, caramel and toffee. These styles put it up front, with hops in a supporting role.

  • Amber ale. Caramel and toast with moderate bitterness. American versions can be fairly hoppy too.
  • Brown ale. Nutty and toffee-like, sometimes with a touch of chocolate. Easygoing and often overlooked.
  • Bock. A strong German lager, rich with bread crust and toasted malt. Doppelbock is bigger and stronger still.

Roasty

Dark roasted malt and barley bring coffee, chocolate and sometimes a burnt edge. Dark doesn't mean heavy: a dry Irish stout is often no stronger than a pale lager.

  • Porter. Chocolate and caramel with softer roast and a medium body.
  • Dry stout. Coffee-like roast and a dry finish, lighter in body than it looks. On nitro, it pours creamy.
  • Milk stout. Brewed with lactose (milk sugar), which adds sweetness and body. Worth knowing if you avoid dairy.
  • Imperial stout. Big, strong and rich, with chocolate, coffee and dark fruit. A beer for sipping slowly.

Sour and tart

Sour beers get their tang from bacteria such as Lactobacillus, and sometimes from wild yeast like Brettanomyces, which adds an earthy note brewers call funk.

  • Berliner weisse. Light, low in alcohol, sharply tart and wheaty. Often served with fruit or syrup.
  • Gose. A tart wheat beer brewed with salt and coriander, so it's sour with a savory edge.
  • Lambic and gueuze. Belgian beers fermented by wild yeast and bacteria from the air, then aged in wood. Gueuze blends young and old lambic for a dry, sharp beer. Kriek (cherry) and framboise (raspberry) are fruit lambics.
  • Wild ales. A broad group, often barrel-aged and often with fruit. Expect tart, fruity and funky in varying amounts.

Fruity and spicy

Here most of the fruit and spice comes from the yeast itself: banana, clove, pepper or bubblegum. Witbier is the exception, with real spice added.

  • Hefeweizen. A cloudy German wheat beer with banana and clove, lively carbonation and very little bitterness.
  • Witbier. A Belgian wheat beer brewed with coriander and orange peel. Light and citrusy.
  • Saison. Dry and highly carbonated, peppery and fruity, sometimes a little tart.
  • Belgian dubbel. Dark and malty, with raisin, plum and caramel and a fairly dry finish.
  • Belgian tripel. Pale, strong and dry, spicy and fruity. The alcohol hides well, so a small pour goes a long way.

If you like this, try that

  • Light lager? Try a kölsch, a helles or a pilsner. Same easy drinking, more flavor.
  • Pilsner? Try a pale ale for a first step into hops, or a West Coast IPA if you like the bitterness.
  • Hazy IPA? Try a hoppy pale ale for the same fruit at lower strength, or a fruited sour if it's the juiciness you like.
  • West Coast IPA? Try a German pilsner for bitterness in a lighter frame.
  • Amber or brown ale? Try a porter for a little roast, or a dubbel for dark fruit.
  • Stout? Try a porter if you want it softer.
  • Hefeweizen? Try a witbier, then a saison.
  • Sours? Start with a Berliner weisse or a gose, then work up to gueuze.
  • Mostly drink wine? Dry sparkling and crisp whites often point toward gueuze, saison or pilsner. Fuller reds point toward dubbel or porter. Going the other way, see wine for beer drinkers.

Use your own ratings to find your lane

Style guides describe an average beer. Your ratings describe you. After a spread of styles, patterns show up: your high scores might cluster around crisp and bitter, while everything roasty sits at a 5.

  1. Rate the ones you don't like too. Low scores teach as much as high ones.
  2. Note flavors, not only styles. Hazy IPA is a label. Juicy and not too bitter is a preference.
  3. Look at what your top scores share, then branch out one step at a time.
  4. Watch strength. If your favorites are all strong, there's usually a lighter style with similar flavors.

Questions

What is the difference between ale and lager?

It comes down to yeast and temperature. Ales ferment warmer with yeast that often adds fruity notes. Lagers ferment cooler and are usually conditioned cold, which gives a cleaner, crisper taste. Color and strength have nothing to do with it: there are pale ales and dark lagers.

What beer should I try if I don't like beer?

Start away from bitterness. A witbier, a hefeweizen, a fruited sour or a smooth dry stout often surprises people who think they don't like beer. Ask for tasters or small pours so you can try a few without committing to a full glass.

What is the least bitter beer style?

Hefeweizen, witbier, helles, most sour beers and milk stouts are all low in bitterness. Hazy IPAs are hoppy but usually taste much softer than other IPAs. If bitterness puts you off, skip West Coast IPAs and double IPAs for now.

What is the difference between a porter and a stout?

Less than you'd think. Both use dark roasted malts. Stouts tend to be roastier and more coffee-like, and porters a little softer and more chocolatey, but brewers use the names loosely. The description on the menu tells you more than the word.

Is a hazy IPA the same as a New England IPA?

Yes. Hazy IPA and New England IPA, or NEIPA, are two names for the same style: cloudy, juicy and full of fruity hop aroma, with a soft body and gentle bitterness.

How do I figure out what beer I like?

Taste a range of styles in small pours, rate each one and note the flavors you noticed. Over time your highest scores will point to a flavor family. Then branch out one step at a time from there.
